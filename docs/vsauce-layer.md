**The seven moves, confirmed on the full corpus**

1. **Object or question in the first sentence.** 27 of 27. The thing is already in frame and he is already asking about it.
2. **He is the one who was wrong, and he shows the path.** "For years I figured they just had little cup-like depressions... but then I did some research and found everyone talking about octahedral insides. But to be sure, I decided to make one of these guys talk." Then the first belief turns out right. The curiosity is a path he walked, and he checks.
3. **Question the question.** "You know what a hole is? What a hole really is? It's a word." "A circle is not a two-dimensional shape." He stops and asks what the key word means, and the episode turns on that.
4. **Answer early, then keep going.** "Did people used to look older? Well, as it turns out, both."
5. **Scale by physical substitution.** A speck of flour is a person, a five-pound bag is everyone alive, a quarter cup is everyone who dies this year. And he does the substitution on camera with real bags of flour.
6. **Name things.** He coins a word for the thing he found ("autoposigrams", "butt head doublers", "posical question beggars") and credits by name the people who found the rest. When someone got there first he adopts their name: "they called it the Vista Paradox. So that's what it should be called."
7. **Deadpan about the body and about himself.** "I am full of bones, and I am proud of that fact." "Now, I won't make a joke about Uranus because some of you have said I do that too often."

And the structural habit: **the last line lands on the viewer or on a joke, never on a summary.**

## 2. How it blends with Cleo's build

| Cleo gives us | Michael adds |
|---|---|
| the beat sheet, the receipts montage, the reframe, the skeptic beat, the optimistic ending | the first-sentence object, the definitional turn, the named credit, the coined word, the physical-substitution scale, the deadpan, the last line on the viewer |
| "I thought X, turns out Y" as a story about the topic | "I thought X... or at least that's what I thought" as a path he walked and then checked |
| chapters as questions | each answer opening a stranger question, so the handoffs are already causal |
| 16-minute episodes with a field trip | 60 to 150 second verticals, one idea, shot at a desk |

They agree on more than they differ: both answer early, both put a human with a question at the centre, both flag what they do not know, both end by widening to "us". Her 25 episodes open inside a scene ("Ready?") 13 times out of 25; his shorts open on the object 27 times out of 27.

**Where his habits collide with our rules, and the ruling**
- *Precision as flex* (bible section 9, tell 8) against his "82.67 microns". Ruling: an exact number is allowed when the number is the point and it arrives with a physical substitution in the same breath. Otherwise round it.
- *"Or is it?"* Ruling: once per episode at most, and only where the honest answer really is "no". He uses it once every nine shorts.
- *Props* against "nothing to do" (bible section 12). His format is an object in the hand. Ruling until the user says otherwise: every script works with zero props. Where an object would help, the script carries an optional `[HOLD: ...]` cue naming something an orthodontist already has on the desk (an aligner, a typodont, an x-ray on a tablet). Holding a thing while talking is not a demo. No self-tests, no stunts, no cutting things open.
- *Tangent chains* against cold-viewer clarity and the 12 to 18 minute band. Ruling: one tangent per episode, one to three sentences, and it has to explain something the viewer needs.
- *"I found this at a thrift shop"* against rule 15. Ruling: the path-he-walked move is only allowed for the narrator's changed mind, which the user confirms. Everything else is credited to named people in the record.
- *The product in the last third.* He can, because the Curiosity Box is his. Ours: the channel's own products only when the user names them, per `docs/voice.md`, and never inside a short's science.

## 3. The short vertical format (his Instagram shape), as a template

Every long-form script ships with two or three standalone shorts written in this shape, not clipped from the long video.

1. **Sentence one: the object, the question, or the flat surprising claim.** 8 to 20 words. No greeting, ever.
2. **The wrong belief, his or the common one.** "You'd think..." "For years I figured..."
3. **The mechanism, one idea, one everyday comparison, ideally a physical substitution.**
4. **The named credit**, when the finding has a finder. Name on screen.
5. **The turn.** One sentence that changes what the first sentence meant.
6. **The last line lands on the viewer or on a deadpan.** Then stop. No call to action, no "follow for more".

150 to 450 words (his median is about 290). One optional `[HOLD]`. One on-screen credit. The checker (`pipeline/edit/ai-tells.mjs`) applies.

## 4. How he films (verified from frames and the reels grid)

What the frame sheets show, short by short:

- **Vertical, phone, handheld, selfie distance.** His face fills the top half of the frame, often cut at the forehead. The object is held up in the bottom third, between his face and the lens, and he looks into the lens, not at the object. (Lock, dice, coin, bones shirt.)
- **Macro inserts of the object.** Every few seconds the cut goes to the thing itself, filling the frame: the dial of the lock, the ball inside the die, the coin spinning. In the die video about half of all frames are hands and object, not face.
- **Several locations in one short, and he moves between them on camera.** The lock short goes pegboard workshop, bookshelf, a hallway with a painting, back to the workshop. The die short goes lounge, table top, a vice, the floor. Each cut to a new room is a beat change.
- **A visual change every 3 to 6 seconds.** On the 3-second frame sheets, adjacent frames are the same shot less than half the time.
- **Text on screen does three jobs.** A hand-lettered label with an arrow on the object ("a Masterlock 'Speed Dial'"); a big number as a title card ("256", "117 billion people"); and a full-frame wall of data as a joke (the entire list of 256 permutations, unreadable on purpose).
- **Sources shown, not cited.** A screenshot of the YouTube page or the paper is held in frame or cut in full-screen (the potato-physics video, the dice mechanism diagram, the Steamboat Willie uploads). Names appear on screen when a person is credited.
- **Face as a reaction shot.** Wide eyes, raised brows, an open mouth, a squint at the object. The reaction is the pattern interrupt.
- **The long-form and the flour short use one wide static shot** at a table in front of a blue wall, with the physical demonstration (bags of flour piling up) happening in that shot, and inserts of paintings and reconstructions cut in full-frame with a caption.
- **Nothing is lit or dressed.** T-shirts, available light, a pegboard, a bookshelf, whatever is behind him. The reels grid confirms it: every thumbnail is his face close to a phone with a thing in his hand, in a workshop or in front of books.

**What Cleo's frames show, for contrast** (`hit-*-sheet.png`): a seated talking head in front of a bookshelf, used for about a quarter of the frames; full-screen flat-colour animation for most of the rest; document screen-recordings with one line highlighted; Zoom interviews with experts framed inside a paper card; archival footage and field b-roll. That is the format Ben's pipeline already builds.

**For Ben, the blend on camera:**
- Long-form stays Cleo's shape: talking head plus animation, documents on screen, no field trip.
- Shorts take Michael's shape: vertical, phone at arm's length, face in the top half, one desk object in the bottom third when a `[HOLD]` cue appears, cut to a macro of the object every few seconds (the animation pipeline can supply the macro when there is no real object), a hand-lettered label with an arrow, one big number as a card, the source's name on screen, and a reaction shot before every turn.
- Two locations per short is enough. The chair-side and the desk.

## 5. Rubric rows added to the bible (22 to 25)

22. **Question the question.** Once per episode the script stops and asks what the key word really means, and the answer changes what the viewer thought the topic was.
23. **Credit by name.** Every finding that came from a person names the person, the place and what they did. At least four named humans per long-form. If someone got there first, say so.
24. **The last line lands on the viewer.** The final substantive sentence before the sign-off is about the viewer's own life or body, or a deadpan, not a summary of the video.
25. **Shorts written, not clipped.** Two or three standalone vertical scripts in the section 3 shape ship with every long-form.
