## 0. What she is actually doing (the one-paragraph version)

Every episode is Cleo, as the audience's proxy, going somewhere physical with one question she genuinely did not know the answer to, finding out in the order a curious person would ask, reacting honestly when a fact breaks her prior model, and coming back with a **reframe**: the thing everyone argues about is not the thing that matters, and here is the thing that does. The optimism is not a tone. It is the reframe. "The most huge-if-true thing here isn't bringing back extinct animals. It's what this means for the animals alive today." "We have made enormous leaps with new cancer treatments, and we have largely ignored the single best way to save the most lives." "The real debate isn't what's a dire wolf. It's what responsibility do we have to the world we created."

Her own rules, in her words: a deep dive into one technology, a real explanation of how it works, genuinely optimistic about the future, and visual in a way that only video can do. Her process: **pitch, info doc, outline, script**, and the script is written in three columns, **ears, eyes, sources**. Visuals come before words. She finds "the visual that helps viewers understand it, maybe a chart, maybe a diagram, maybe a piece of archival footage" first, then writes to it.

## 2. The beat sheet (positions as % of runtime)

**0 to 3 %: Inside a scene, present tense, holding the thing.**
"Ready?" "Okay ready?" "That right there is the TOE bone from a BABY dinosaur! And I'm holding it." "I'm wearing the real Team USA Olympic uniforms." "I'm about to go into surgery. I'm feeling really nervous." "This is my dog, Thor." "Ok. I need to show you something: This is a nuclear reactor built in 1962."
Then one impossible fact with a human-scale comparison in the same breath: "That hole is deeper than the deepest part of the ocean. It's deeper than Mount Everest is tall." "You would have to stack the tallest building in the world six times."
No greeting. No name. No show name until the montage ends.

**3 to 6 %: The question stack.** Three questions, escalating from the naive to the frontier, verbatim in this shape:
"If you dug a hole all the way to the center of the Earth, what would you find? What would happen to you? And what does our newest tech tell us is really going on down below our feet?"
"If you fell into a black hole, what would actually happen to you? What would it feel like? And what does our newest science tell us is really happening inside?"
"Why do this? How does egg freezing actually work? And what's the future here, how could this be huge if true?"
Pattern: **what happens / what does it feel like / what does the newest science say**.

**6 to 20 %: The receipts montage.** Six to twelve clips, news anchors and movies, no commentary, ending on a punchline clip ("that's wild!", "Nature doesn't work that way." "It's the most wonderful thing."). The montage does the job of "everyone is talking about this" so she never has to say it.

**Right after the montage: the personal admission and the promise.**
"I've loved dinosaurs since I was a kid but it turns out a lot of what I thought I knew is actually wrong or at least outdated."
"I remember hearing all about the ozone hole as a kid. And then one day the news about it just stopped... and I stopped thinking about it."
"What I didn't realize was that cancer is a problem for our generation in a way that it wasn't for previous ones."
Then one promise sentence with three concrete deliverables: "I'm going to give you rare access into a warehouse full of dinosaur bones, go out into the field and try to discover my own fossil, and show you something new these scientists just discovered." Rare access, try it myself, something new. That is the promise formula. It is said once.

**20 to 40 %: The vehicle and the descent.** Every episode has a vehicle that lets her move through the topic in order: an imaginary drilling machine, an imaginary spaceship, a drill through the ice, a recipe with three paths, a treadmill in a heat chamber, a syringe. The vehicle turns an abstract topic into a journey with a "deeper" direction. "Time to go deeper." "Let's go." "So let's go deeper." "Now we're crossing into the mantle." Each stop is one mechanism, one visual, one plain-word name ("cut, poison, burn, melt"; "the upper, the outsole, the midsole").

**The dumb question, asked on our behalf.** Every episode has one: "But hang on... how do we know that? If humans have only ever been 12 km down, how do we know that?" "But I had a much more basic question about all of this: Why is the earth in layers?" "What qualifies as a dinosaur?" "But wait, how could there be liquid water under all this ice?" The expert answers it, and the answer is always the best part of the episode.

**Expert introduction formula.** Quote first, then: "That's Dr Megan Newcombe, a geologist and vulcanologist at the University of Maryland." Name, title, institution, and when possible one human detail: "She's actually operated on me before. She's seen my insides. We're close." "Yeah. Greg has leukemia." Experts are people with questions, never authorities with answers.

**The wonder spike (35 to 50 %).** A fact breaks her model and she says so on camera, in the moment: "WHAT. That's an amazing fact. I had no idea. The stegosaurus was an ancient relic to the T-Rex! My whole childhood was a lie." "I don't know why I assumed it had to be linear but the fact that it's curved blows my mind." "So what you're telling me is there's a barcode for our magnetic fields at the bottom of the ocean? Excellent." "Wait, 0.1 %. That's actually a lot genetically."

**Sponsor (36 to 54 %).** Always right after a cliffhanger she has planted: "They're about to let me look for some dinosaur bones. I've been waiting for this moment my whole entire life. But first let me show you something." "We're past the event horizon. But before I show you what's inside this black hole, let me show you something here on Earth." Then "Now back to the story." The ad is in her voice and tied to the episode's theme when possible.

**The reframe (45 to 65 %).** The topic pivots from what people argue about to what matters. It is delivered as a discovery she made, not a thesis she brought: "At this point in the episode I was expecting to be talking about cancer vaccines... my whole understanding of this topic just shifted." "I think it's a distraction from what is the real huge-if-true use of this technology."

**The skeptic beat (55 to 75 %).** The strongest objection, stated fairly, with the technical name: "By the way, what I'm doing is controversial." "There are good reasons to be cautious here. This is lead time bias. This is overdiagnosis bias." "People online were really quick to point out that these actually aren't really dire wolves at all. And they're right." "I should emphasize as a health warning here: this is ongoing research and there is no agreed upon picture." She resolves what can be resolved and leaves open what is open: "We don't really know until we try."

**The friction and the small jokes.** She is never smooth. "I'm gonna get out now. This is the amount of time that I want to spend underground." "If I lose a finger, it's my fault." "I'm reaching a point where I can't talk casually anymore." "Well, there goes my home." "Might also be a bone of like a large chicken." "Oh no I finished my Oreos." The jokes are always at her own expense or at the situation, never at a person.

**The cutting edge (75 to 90 %).** "Now the cutting edge is..." Dates, names, what is being tried this year. "In 2019 a series of papers were published." "A team from Europe has found a core, 1.3 million years, and they haven't yet analyzed it." "They're testing it right now."

**The ending (90 to 100 %), in this exact order:**
1. Zoom out to the species: "We are a curious species locked on the outside of our own home and we're doing everything we can to peek inside." "Every dinosaur in every museum is a stone replica of what was once a living breathing animal." "We are living in a world that we changed before we understood the consequences."
2. Zoom back in to her: "I'm so glad that that little guy and I are alive at the same time." "Right now what I'm most feeling is grateful."
3. One action or one choice, never a slogan: "Ask your doctor about what tests you might benefit from and when. I know I'm glad I did." "The next big choice."
4. Outro: what the show is ("an optimistic show about using technology to make the world better"), subscribe, "I'd love to meet you in the comments", "see you for the next one", then a blooper. The subscribe ask is after the story is over. Never inside it.

## 3. Sentence-level fingerprints (copy the moves, not the words)

- **Present tense for the journey, past tense for the history.** "We're now crossing into a liquid soup of metals." "It's April 5th, 1950."
- **Scale by household object, always.** Fuel pellet = finger to the first joint. Bone texture = Aero chocolate bar. Lake breakthrough = Mentos in a Pepsi bottle. Ocean cleanup = a robot vacuum. Research tool = "like Clippy but way smarter." One comparison per number, never two.
- **Triple for motion, never for rhetoric.** "deeper and deeper and deeper", "faster and faster and faster", "dig and dig and dig", "bigger and bigger and bigger". She never writes "It's bold. It's brave. It's beautiful."
- **Plain-word taxonomies.** "Cut, poison, burn, melt." "Snow, firn, ice." "Back breeding, cloning, gene editing." Name the category in one syllable if you can.
- **The prior, then the correction.** "I always heard that to figure out how old your dog was you multiply by 7. Turns out, not how this works!" "I thought it was THE big fertility issue and that's not true."
- **The stat that got me.** "Here's the stat that really got me: half of all men and a third of all women in the US will be diagnosed with some form of cancer." Then what it means for you, specifically: "if you think this topic doesn't apply to you, specifically you, you're wrong. And me."
- **I don't say that to scare you.** Fear is acknowledged and redirected in one sentence. "I don't say that to scare you. I say it because..."
- **Try to decide before I tell you.** "Sticky glue that makes it easier to catch a football: okay or not okay?" Audience participation with an answer thirty seconds later.
- **The recipe.** "Imagine you're a mad scientist. Here's the recipe. Step one, choose your animal." Processes are recipes with numbered steps and named ingredients.
- **Flag the simplification instead of faking precision.** "I'm paraphrasing here a lot." "These are general numbers. Specific types differ wildly." "Experts differ on the exact number here but they agree that..."
- **Disclose everything.** "By the way, they didn't sponsor this video or anything. You're always going to know when stuff is sponsored."
- **Precise date, precise place, ordinary day.** "It started 66.04 million years ago on a normal day in the dinosaur kingdom except that a speck appeared in the sky."
- **Movie as shared memory.** Jurassic Park, Interstellar, Godzilla, Predator. "To be fair to Jurassic Park, they didn't know that."
- **"Let me show you" is the only promise verb.** Not "we'll explore", not "let's dive in", not "buckle up".
- **Reaction words she actually uses:** wild, insane, nutty, awesome, so cool, "oh my god", "WHAT.", "Excellent.", "Yikes.", "Ooof." Never "mind-blowing", never "game-changer".
- **The pivot words:** "But hang on." "But here's the thing." "Turns out," "Wait." "Okay." "So." "Time to go deeper." "Now, to the truly insane."
- **No aphorisms, no taglines, no puns.** Her closing lines are plain declaratives about humans: "but man, we try so hard."

## 4. The wonder toolkit (Nye, Tyson, Sagan/Druyan, and Cleo with Brian Cox)

**Bill Nye: one sentence, four costumes.** "Wind comes from the heating and cooling of the Earth by the sun and the Earth's rotation." He says it in the cold open, in the plastic wind box, in the sketch, in the song, at the landing. A kid can repeat it after one viewing. Rule for us: **the episode has one sentence a twelve-year-old can repeat, and it is said at least three times in three different scenes.** He also proves every claim with a household demo (toaster for rising air, freezer door for sinking air, oil in a tipped dish for pressure). Rule: **every mechanism gets a thing you can do at home or a thing Ben can do in the clinic.**

**Neil deGrasse Tyson: make the ordinary strange, then climb a ladder.** "It's not that there's nothing between me and you. Air is blocking the view, except air happens to be transparent to the light we detect." He starts from the thing you never questioned, checks each rung ("Agreed?", "Okay?", "With me?"), and builds to a payoff where the listener says it back in their own words ("So I'm not getting irradiated because the waves can't get through the holes." "Correct."). Rule: **start each chapter from something the viewer already believes and make it strange in one sentence. End each chapter with the viewer able to explain it back.** His ending, and hers with Brian Cox, are the same: "the more you understand about nature, the more mysterious and magical it becomes." Wonder increases with knowledge. The script must make the viewer smarter and more curious, never smarter and done.

**Sagan/Druyan: go back to the moment you did not understand.** Ann Druyan on Carl Sagan: when explaining something, "he would go back to that time when he didn't understand it" and retrace the steps. And: "it was easiest to convey the information in the context of the life of the scientist, or in the context of our own personal experience." This is exactly Cleo's "a 30-year-old scientist with a question" and "I remember as a kid." Rule: **every mechanism is told either as the moment a named person figured it out, or as the moment the narrator stopped being wrong about it.** Tyson's phrase for the target: "if you have a beating heart, that's the audience."

**Tyson's three MasterClass moves, applied:** know your audience (Ben's is parents and teens, so the comparisons are lunchboxes, pencil erasers, sports, phones); give less information to create curiosity (open a loop, delay the answer 60 to 90 seconds); put numbers in context with a comparison that shames or delights ("Americans spend more on lip balm than on this mission").

## 6. The self-judging comparison (what "hand it to Cleo" means in practice)

When a draft is done, lay it beside the closest real episode (a body-topic script goes beside Cancer and Egg Freezing; a myth-bust beside Nuclear Waste; a journey beside Digging and Antarctica) and answer, in writing:
1. Where does her cold open land its first impossible fact, and where does ours? (hers: sentence 2 or 3)
2. How many words before her first named human? Ours?
3. Her sponsor cliffhanger versus ours, side by side.
4. Her reframe sentence versus ours, side by side. Does ours arrive as a discovery?
5. Her skeptic beat's technical term versus ours.
6. Her ending's four moves versus ours, in order.
7. Ten random sentences from each, read aloud. Which ones would she cut?
Then rewrite the losers. Repeat until no side-by-side pair is obviously hers.

## 7. The process (pitch, info doc, outline, script), as we run it

1. **Pitch**: title in one of her shapes (see `docs/explainer-format.md`), thumbnail words, and the one-sentence huge-if-true claim ("if this is true, it changes X for everyone who Y").
2. **Info doc**: research with sources. Must find, before outlining: the hero object to hold; the "stat that got me"; the dumb question; two or three named humans (one who figured it out, one who does it now, one who is skeptical); the strongest objection with its technical name; the reframe; the one sentence a twelve-year-old can repeat; the cutting-edge item with a date; the household demo. If any of those is missing the research is not done.
3. **Outline**: the beat sheet in section 2 with target percentages and word counts at the host's measured pace.
4. **Script**: three columns, ears / eyes / sources, then flattened to `script.md` with sparse `[VISUAL:]` notes per `docs/voice.md`.
5. **Judge**: score with section 5, compare with section 6, rewrite. Ship only with the scorecard attached.

## 9. Human, not AI: the tells and the fix

Added 2026-09-17 after the user asked for scripts that could not be identified as machine-written. These are the habits a model falls into when it writes "punchy". Cleo and Ben do none of them at this density. Run the checker (`pipeline/edit/ai-tells.mjs <script.md>`) and then read the whole thing aloud once more.

**The tells (hunt every one):**
1. **The negation reframe.** "It's not X. It's Y." "That's not a discount. That's a different purchase." "Not the company. The shoebox." One per episode is a human move. Five is a signature.
2. **The fragment drumroll.** "On purpose." "Nothing." "Gone." "For teeth." Ending every paragraph on a two-word fragment reads as a trailer. Humans finish their sentences most of the time, and when they cut one short it is because they got distracted, not because it was dramatic.
3. **The parallel stack.** "The building, yes. The markup, please. The monthly drive, probably." Three matched clauses in a row with the same shape. A person would say the first one, then ramble the other two.
4. **The self-aware pointer.** "Read that again." "Let that sink in." "Here's the thing." "And this is the part nobody tells you." Delete all of them; if the fact is good the pointer is not needed.
5. **The engineered callback.** The comparison in the open that "pays off" in the third act. Real people repeat themselves by accident, not by design. One deliberate callback per episode, never announced.
6. **The sincerity markers as seasoning.** "Genuinely", "actually", "honestly", "literally" every other sentence. Ben uses them, but as connective tissue at two or three per paragraph, and never two in one sentence.
7. **The tidy aphorism as paragraph closer.** "A photo sees your crown, and everything that went wrong happened at the root." Keep one per episode, put it where it is earned, and let the rest of the paragraphs trail off the way speech does.
8. **Precision as flex.** "Seventeen days after." "Six thousand eight hundred and ninety-four." A person says "about two and a half weeks" and "almost seven thousand" unless the exact number is the point, and then says "I looked this up" or "I'm reading this off the filing".
9. **Everything in the same register.** Every paragraph the same length, the same shape, the same rhythm. Human speech has a long messy run-on, then a short one, then a digression, then "anyway".
10. **No mistakes, no hedges.** A person says "I could be botching this", "I think it was October, maybe November", "I'm gonna get this slightly wrong". A model states everything with the same confidence.

**The fix, in order:**
- Rewrite every negation reframe as a plain statement or as a spoken correction ("well, it's not really a discount, it's a whole different thing you're buying").
- Join the fragments back onto the sentence before them with "and", "which", "so".
- Break every parallel stack: keep the first clause, ramble the rest.
- Delete the pointers.
- Add one hedge or self-correction per chapter, only where it is true.
- Vary paragraph length on purpose: one run-on of forty words, then one of eight.
- Read it aloud. Anywhere you would not say it to a friend across a table, say it to the friend and write down what came out.

**The test:** hand ten random paragraphs from the script and ten from the host's real transcripts to someone who has not seen either. If they can sort them better than a coin flip, rewrite.

## 10. Algorithm fit: confirm the click, then keep the promise

Added 2026-09-17. Sources: the MrBeast production memo (the first minute "demonstrates that their expectations from the thumbnail will be met"; a wow moment at 30 seconds "because who else on YouTube can do that"; losing a third of viewers in minute one is "reasonably good"), Paddy Galloway ("the thumbnail and title create an open loop in the brain we want to close... the first hook, not your intro"; breadcrumb every two minutes; ten points of retention is the difference between 100K and 1M), YouTube's own guidance (nothing before the content; early retention "one of the strongest signals of quality"; since April 2026 satisfaction surveys, shares, repeat views and 7-day returns are weighted above raw watch time, and padding is penalised), 1of10 and Prepublish (retention typically 65 to 80% at 0:30; above 70% is good, above 80% is exceptional; four or more specific claims in the opening is a strong hook), and Cleo's own openings (Digging: the title words are the first sentence; Shoe: the thumbnail action, cutting the shoe, is the first sentence; Antarctica: "there's something hiding down below this ice" by 0:10).

`docs/youtube-playbook.md` section 2 still holds (no intro, no logo, first 30 seconds, re-hook every 60 to 90 seconds, end screen last 15 seconds). This section is the script-level checklist on top of it.

**The opening, second by second**
| by | the script must | why |
|---|---|---|
| 0:05 | put the thumbnail's image on screen and its words in the host's mouth | the viewer is checking whether the video is the thing they clicked; a mismatch is the single biggest cause of the 30-second cliff |
| 0:10 | say the title, or its key words, in a sentence that also carries a stake | "confirm the click": the title is a question the viewer wants closed, so name it before they wonder if they are in the wrong video |
| 0:15 | make one specific promise with a number in it | "specific beats vague"; four concrete claims in the opening reads as a strong hook, fewer than three reads as weak |
| 0:20 to 0:30 | plant the first open loop that the body will close (the question stack does this) | the commitment hook; the viewer decides here whether to give you the video |
| 0:25 to 0:35 | a hard pattern interrupt (the receipts montage: cuts, music, other voices) | the drift point for new viewers; a change of stimulus resets attention |
| 0:30 to 0:60 | the wow moment: the one visual only this channel can show | MrBeast's crane at 30 seconds; ours is the root with the bone going dark |
| 0:45 | the first real fact the viewer did not know | value must arrive before the first minute ends or the click was not rewarded |

**The body**
- **Breadcrumb every two minutes.** Each chapter boundary is a spoken question and the previous chapter ends by pointing at it ("hang onto that, it comes back"). Never more than 2:30 without a new loop opened.
- **No dull moments.** Every 60 to 90 seconds contains at least one of: a new fact, a visual change, a reaction from the host, a named person, or a number with a comparison. If a 90-second stretch has none, cut it.
- **Close every loop you open.** The thumbnail words, the title question, and every "in a minute I'll show you" must each be answered explicitly and audibly. Unanswered loops are what the satisfaction survey punishes.
- **Natural length.** Do not pad to a runtime. Since April 2026 padding suppresses distribution. The word budget in `docs/voice.md` is a ceiling, not a target.
- **Spoken search.** The primary search phrase is said in the first 15 seconds and once in the last minute. Chapter questions are said verbatim.

**The ending**
- **The share moment.** One line or image in the last 90 seconds that a viewer would send to someone ("they told people to keep paying"). Shares are weighted roughly five to eight times a like.
- **One action, then the sign-off.** The action makes the video useful (a satisfaction signal). A specific next-video pointer feeds session time, but only when the video exists; pointing at a video that is not made is a fabrication (rubric 21). Otherwise end the way Cleo does: the plate and "see you on the next one", and let the end screen carry a real published video.
- **End screen only in the last 10 to 15 seconds,** after the story is finished.

**Benchmarks to check after publish:** 30-second retention above 70% (above 80% is exceptional); the retention curve should have no cliff after the opening, only a slope; the first spike on the curve should sit on the wow moment.

**Tell number 11, added after the second read of the SmileDirectClub script: the paragraph button.** Every paragraph ending on a quip, a punch, or a one-line summary ("Nobody's teeth ever got straighter because of a lobby." "For teeth." "It drifts."). The checker cannot count this because the buttons are full sentences. Read the last sentence of every paragraph in a column. If more than one in three is a punchline or a summary, the script is machine-shaped. A person ends most paragraphs on the fact, or mid-thought, and lands a button maybe four or five times in a whole video. Related: the same reveal framing reused ("this is the one that got me", "this is where it flipped for me", "the part I didn't see coming"). One per episode.

## 11. Story spine and the ending (rewritten after the SmileDirectClub v7 read)

The chapters are questions because search needs them to be. That is not a story. Three tools make it one.

**1. ABT at every scale (Randy Olson, "Houston, We Have a Narrative").** And, but, therefore. "For a hundred years braces meant an office AND an x-ray, BUT a company deleted the office and the x-ray with it, THEREFORE two million people had bone moving in their jaw with nobody watching." Write the whole episode as one ABT sentence before outlining, then write each chapter as its own ABT. If a chapter has only ANDs it is a list. If the boundary between two chapters does not contain a BUT or a THEREFORE (or their spoken forms: "hang onto that", "which brings me to", "so"), it is a list break and the viewer feels it.

**2. Three acts, and the narrator's mind changes (Johnny Harris; Cleo in every episode).** Act one: a question the host genuinely holds. Act two: the journey, where the host is the viewer's proxy and says out loud when a fact breaks their model. Act three: the answer, plus the thing the host now thinks that they did not think at the start. Cleo's is always visible: "I thought I knew dinosaurs, I was wrong." "I was expecting to talk about cancer vaccines, my whole understanding shifted." Ours: "I thought teeth would end this company. It was a sentence in a bill."

**3. Chronology forward, once.** When the topic has a timeline, walk it forward and do not double back. A history aside (1896) goes inside the body where it explains something, as one sentence. It does not go in the ending.

**What not to do (the shoebox mistake).** Do not invent a prop mid-script and make it the spine. A returning element has to be something the viewer saw in the first minute: the hero object, the note, the opening questions. A new object introduced at 70% and "resolved" at 95% reads as the writer's device, not the story, and the viewer asks "wait, what shoebox?"

**Her endings, measured (last 100 to 170 words of eight transcripts).** They are short and they all do the same five things in roughly this order:
1. **Answer the opening question in one plain line.** "We found life in one of the coldest, darkest, most alien places on Earth."
2. **Move the question up one level, to us.** "The real debate about de-extinction isn't what's a dire wolf. It's what responsibility do we have to the world we created." "The whole point of technology is to make people better... how far and how fast is the big debate we're going to keep on having far beyond sports." "It's not that people should get tested all the time. It's that in the push for new treatments we've ignored a lot of people."
3. **Say a feeling, plainly, in the first person.** "And now I love them." "I'm so glad that little guy and I are alive at the same time." "And personally I can't wait to see what humans are capable of."
4. **Optimism about what is next**, with a real thing. "This is the Golden Age of paleontology and there is still so much that we don't know." "We are a curious species locked on the outside of our own home and we're doing everything we can to peek inside."
5. **One takeaway, when the topic is the viewer's body.** "Ask your doctor about what tests you might benefit from and when. I know I'm glad I did."
Then the plate (what the show is, subscribe, "see you for the next one"), and a blooper. The plate is after the story, never inside it.

Ours, in the same order: answer the three opening questions in a line each; "the real fight was never cheap versus expensive, it was what you're allowed to skip"; "that kind of gets me, honestly"; the cheaper-braces future is real and the exam and x-ray stay in; see a dentist and get an x-ray; then the sign-off, with no specific next video unless one exists.

**Test for the ending:** read only the last 300 words to someone who has not seen the video. They should be able to say what the video was about, what the host thinks now, and what to do. If they ask about an object they never saw, cut the object.

## 12. Nothing invented, nothing to do (added after the SmileDirectClub v8 read)

The user's rule, in their words: no fabricated info, nothing "from me" that I did not give you, nothing I have to go physically do. It should be a compelling narrative to explain something.

**Three sources of fabrication a script can smuggle in, and the rule for each:**
1. **Facts.** Every number, date, quote and claim traces to research.md or to a source listed in the script's "facts verified" block. If the writer cannot point at the source, the sentence goes. Allegations are always framed as allegations.
2. **The host's life.** The host is a real person. The script may use what is generic to anyone in the profession ("I move teeth all day", "when you come into my office", "I prescribe aligners"). It may use opinions that follow from the brief and the channel's stated positions. It may not invent a specific patient, event, memory, habit, possession or number ("the first patient who showed me the ad", "people started showing up at my office with a shoebox", "I use remote monitoring myself", "I had to go back and check that number"). Cleo's personal beats work because they are hers. Ours are only allowed when the user supplies them. When a beat needs a human witness and the user has not supplied one, attribute it to the documented group ("orthodontists all over the country", with the clip or survey that shows it).
3. **The host's body.** The host talks to a camera. No demo, no self-test, no field trip, no prop he has to hold. Every "I tried it myself" beat from Cleo's format becomes a narrated descent with animation.

**Hedges.** A hedge that is a live speech act ("I could be pronouncing that wrong", "I think") is fine. A hedge that claims a past action ("I had to read that twice", "I went back and checked") is a fabricated event and is not.

**The mechanism.** Every script carries a list, near the top, of every first-person line it contains, each tagged generic, opinion-from-brief, or user-supplied. The host confirms or cuts each one before recording. If the list is missing, the script is not done.

**What "compelling narrative to explain something" means here.** The story is the topic's own: the company, the mechanism, the law, the people documented in the record. The narrator's arc is the one thing that is allowed to be his: what he expected and what turned out to be true, and only if the user confirms it.

## 13. The five added after the SmileDirectClub v9 read

**Cold-viewer clarity.** The writer lives inside the script for hours and starts using its motifs as words ("the look", "the sling", "the doorbell"). A viewer who arrived from a thumbnail has no idea what "the look" is. Rule: a coined phrase is allowed only after its plain meaning has been said in the same sentence, and when it returns after more than two minutes it gets the plain meaning again. The ending uses no shorthand at all, because people skip to endings. Test: read only the ending to someone cold. Every noun should be a real noun.

**Repeatable sentence.** From Nye (section 4). Now scored, because a script can have the beat sheet right and still leave the viewer with nothing to say at dinner.

**The argument.** Cleo's most-shared episodes make the viewer decide something ("okay or not okay?", "would you use an artificial womb?"). The comments then argue about the thing, which is a satisfaction and session signal. Ours: the checklist game, plus an honest "if you'd draw the line somewhere else, tell me" near the end.

**Humor as seasoning.** Ben's channel is funny. The human-rhythm pass (section 9) stripped quips, which was right, but it also stripped the laughs. The difference: a quip is a paragraph button with no image; a laugh is a specific picture or a reaction ("I take longer than that picking a sandwich", "which I'm not going to pretend I didn't enjoy a little"). Three to five per episode, never at a person, never two in one paragraph.

**Chapter answers up front.** Search indexes chapters as separate results and AI Overviews quote one complete sentence. A chapter titled with a question that only answers it at the end gives the index nothing. Answer in the first sentence, then explain. The exception is a chapter that is a game or a reveal, and the script says so.

**Tell number 12, added after the SmileDirectClub v10 read: the telegraphic instruction.** The takeaway written as a list of noun phrases or a "Not because X. Just because Y." pair: "One exam, one x-ray, somebody with a license. Not because something's definitely wrong. Just because nobody's looked under your gum line yet." No person gives advice like that. Advice is a full sentence addressed to one person, with a verb, and it admits what the speaker does not know: "Go see a dentist, get an x-ray, and let somebody look at what's going on under your gums, because nobody has yet. It might be totally fine. But you don't know that, and honestly neither do I." Test: say the takeaway to a friend across a table. If you would not say it that way, it is not written yet. This applies to the pinned comment too.

## 14. No phantom promises (added after the SmileDirectClub v11 read)

The close of v11 said "why a tooth hurts on day one and quits by day four, that's the next one." No such video exists or was planned. That is a fabrication aimed at the viewer, and it also breaks the satisfaction signal: a viewer who goes looking for the promised video and does not find it does not come back.

**The rule.** Any sentence that points forward has to point at something real:
- a published video on the channel, named by title, with its id noted in the script file;
- a script in `videos/<slug>/` that the user has said is being produced;
- text that is written in this same file (the pinned comment, the description).
If none of those exist, the video ends on itself. The last lines restate what the video was about in the host's plain words and use the generic sign-off ("keep flossing, I'll see you on the next one"), which promises nothing specific. The end screen can carry a real published video without the script naming one.

**Where this hides.** "That's the next one." "I'll do a whole video on that." "Part two." "Link in the description" for a link nobody has written. "Sources in the pinned comment" when no pinned comment is drafted. "My company is working on this" when the user has not said so. Sweep the script for every future-tense reference before scoring.

**Why Cleo gets away with "we're making another episode about the ozone layer" at the end of Antarctica:** it was already in production and came out two weeks later. The reference was real. Ours was not.

## 16. The blind test (added 2026-09-22): the gate everything else feeds

The user's actual bar: a normal person, shown our paragraphs mixed with real ones, cannot tell which is which. Every earlier rule is a heuristic for passing this test. This is the test.

**Tool.** `node pipeline/edit/blind-test.mjs --script videos/<slug>/script.md --n 12 --seed 7` writes four files to `videos/<slug>/blind/`:
- `quiz.json`: 24 paragraphs, 12 from the script and 12 real, shuffled, unlabeled. The real ones are re-chunked on sentence boundaries to 45 to 110 words so chunk edges are not a clue, and weighted toward Ben's own transcripts (half), then Cleo (a quarter), then Michael long-form (a quarter), so topic is not a clue either.
- `key.json`: the labels.
- `quiz.html`: a self-scoring page for a person. Open it, pick Human or AI for each, press Score.
- `judge-prompt.md`: the exact prompt for a model judge.

**Procedure.**
1. Generate the quiz.
2. Launch three fresh judges (different models, no context, no access to the script or the key) with only `judge-prompt.md`. Each writes a verdict JSON.
3. `node pipeline/edit/blind-test.mjs --score videos/<slug>/blind/key.json videos/<slug>/blind/verdict-*.json` prints each judge's accuracy, how many script paragraphs they caught, and every caught paragraph with the judges' stated reasons.
4. Rewrite the caught paragraphs against the stated reasons. Do not rewrite paragraphs that passed.
5. Regenerate with a new seed so the judges see a different sample, and run again. Ship when every judge is under 60% and no paragraph is caught by all three.
6. Give the person `quiz.html` too. The model judges are the rehearsal; the person is the performance.

**Why three judges and a threshold of 60%, not 50%.** One judge on 24 items is noisy; 60% is about where a result stops being luck. Three judges with different models catch different tells. A paragraph flagged by all three is a real tell regardless of the overall score.

**What to do with the reasons.** The judges' one-line reasons are the most useful output. They name the features a reader actually uses. Add any new recurring reason to the tell list in section 9.

**Tells 13 to 18, from the first blind test (2026-09-22).** These are the reasons the strongest judge gave when it sorted every one of our paragraphs from the real ones. They are what a good reader actually uses.
13. **The roadmap tricolon.** "Let me walk you through X, Y, and the one Z that..." A person promises one thing, vaguely.
14. **The recap that answers the opening questions in turn.** An outline device. A person circles back to one of them, if any.
15. **Engineered symmetry.** "Same push, most of the signal gone, most of the movement gone." "They cut X and Y and Z, but they also cut the one..." Real speech is lopsided.
16. **The sincerity tag.** "I mean that." "Honestly." "I want to be clear." Used as authenticity signalling, they read as the opposite. One per script.
17. **The placed joke.** A one-liner sitting on its own sentence after a fact ("I take longer than that picking a sandwich."). A person's joke is inside the sentence, as an aside, mid-thought.
18. **Spelled-out numerals and date-stamped staccato.** "Four point eight million dollars." "October 24th, 2024, Dentsply Sirona suspends Byte worldwide." Captions render speech as digits, and a person embeds the date in a sentence ("so then in October of 2024 they..."). Write numbers as digits and dates inside clauses.
And one from the Sonnet judge: **the closing zinger** ("Everybody else is in line behind the banks."), a composed ironic button that reads written, not recalled. Covered by tell 11, repeated here because it survived two rewrites.

**Tells 19 to 26, from blind-test rounds two and three (2026-09-22).** After the paragraph buttons and roadmaps were gone, three judges still sorted every paragraph. Their reasons had moved down a level, to the moves of composed persuasive writing. Select against all of these.
19. **The pre-empted objection.** "I know what some of you are thinking, which is..." "I know the bone thing sounds dramatic, but..." Mind-reading the audience and answering them is essay craft. A person just says the thing.
20. **Self-aware honesty framing.** "If I pretend otherwise I'm kind of insulting you." "'You need to see a dentist' is exactly what a dentist would say." Commenting on your own rhetorical position reads as copy.
21. **Thesis and antithesis.** "The real fight was never X. The fight was Y." "Straightening teeth isn't really the job. The job is..." The negation-definition move, in any wording.
22. **Mirrored mechanism.** "On the squished side... on the stretched side." Two-sided explanations in matched clauses with no backtracking. A person explains one side, wanders, and comes back to the other side later.
23. **Packaged metaphor with its tag.** "The plastic is kind of just the doorbell, is the way I think about it." "Telling your body, hey, eat the bone." Personification and analogies that arrive complete, especially with an attribution tag.
24. **The isolated number sentence.** "And it was $1,895." "For teeth." A figure or a fragment given its own sentence for timing.
25. **Signposting.** "Let me walk you through." "I'll get to why." "Which brings me back to." Forward and backward references to the structure of the talk.
26. **The curated escalation.** Figures or dates in an ascending series that lands on a superlative, with no fumbling or approximation.
The pattern across 19 to 26: every one is a thing a good writer does on purpose. A speaker does them by accident, at most one per minute, and never in a stack. The rule that covers all of them: one deliberate move per 300 words, and the rest of the words just carry information.

**Calibration, learned the hard way (2026-09-22, five rounds).** A strong model judge (Opus) sorted our paragraphs from real transcripts at 100% in every round, including after the paragraph buttons, roadmaps, symmetry and sincerity tags were gone, and including a round against scripted creators only. Then two control runs, where the "AI" half was secretly Cleo's own paragraphs, showed what the judge was actually doing: it called every Cleo paragraph human because of leftover speech artifacts (a "spaceime" caption typo, scrambled word order, spliced quotes, missing commas, "my whole entire life"), and the one Cleo paragraph with no artifacts, the LIGO cold open, it flagged as AI for "perfectly nested cadence with no disfluency". So in clean text, a read-aloud script by Cleo herself would fail this judge. The judge detects composed-versus-transcribed, and a script is composed by definition.

**What that means for the rules:**
1. The Opus text test is a tell harvester, not a gate. Its reasons are gold (tells 13 to 26 came from it). Its accuracy number is not the bar.
2. The gate is a weaker reader, calibrated against a control. Run the same judge (Sonnet, or a person on `quiz.html`) on two quizzes: ours, and a control where the "AI" half is Cleo (`--control`). Pass when the judge's accuracy on ours is within 15 points of their accuracy on the control. If a person catches Cleo at 60% and us at 65%, we are at her level, which is the actual goal.
3. The true test is the recorded transcript. Once Ben has read the script on camera, run his transcript of it against his transcripts of his own unscripted videos. That is what a viewer meets, and it is the only version of the test where disfluency is fairly distributed.
4. Keep the cleaning on. `normalize()` strips quotes, speaker labels, ellipses, um/uh, stutters up to three words, shouting, and lowercase-after-period, so the judges have as little artifact as possible to lean on. Some always survives; that is why the control exists.

## 17. Style prompts (added 2026-09-22)

After six blind-test rounds the user's own read still sorted the script at 21 of 24, and the honest conclusion was that composed prose reads as composed no matter how many tells you remove. The user proposed the fix: a style prompt, the way Suno takes a style for a track. The user writes a short brief describing how the person talks. The script is rewritten against it. The facts, sources, verbatim legal lines and the no-fabrication rules never change with the style; only the voice does.

**Where they live.** `docs/style-prompts/<name>.md`. A script names its prompt in the header as `style: <name>`. Every later pass on that script, and every new script for the same host, uses the same prompt unless the user changes it.

**The first one.** `kitchen-table` (the user's, 2026-09-22): no commas at all, no exclamation points, contractions always, fragments allowed, no lists of three, no summaries, no tidy moral, no hedging words, specific over general, talk to one person, open mid-thought, a new reason to keep watching every twenty seconds. Its machine checks are listed at the bottom of the prompt file. Under it the checker runs with `--allow-fragments`.

**How a prompt interacts with the rest of this bible.** The beat sheet (section 2), the algorithm layer (section 10), the story spine (section 11), rule 15 (nothing invented) and the blind test (section 16) all still apply. The prompt replaces the sentence-level guidance in sections 9 and 13 wherever they conflict, because the prompt is the user's ear and the sections were my inference of it. Where the prompt is silent, sections 9 and 13 stand.

**Style prompt result, kitchen-table (2026-09-22, rounds 7 and 8).** The blind test scored the no-comma, short-sentence version worse than the version before it: Sonnet judges 88% and 67%, then 71% and 58% with commas equalized, against 42% and 54% for v18 and a 42% baseline on Cleo. The judges named the rhythm itself: "exaggerated performance of punchy short sentences." Lesson for all future prompts: any rule that makes every sentence the same shape becomes a fingerprint. Real transcribed speech averages 12 to 16 words a sentence with a wide spread. The bans in a prompt (no exclamation points, no lists of three, no summaries, no moral, contractions, specifics) transfer well; rhythm rules do not.

**Locked (2026-09-22).** The house voice is `docs/style-prompts/bentist-casual.md`: the kitchen-table prompt with the comma rule relaxed to numbers and item lists and the register pushed to casual near-slang. It is the default for every script and is wired into the scriptwriter agent, the /script skill and CLAUDE.md.

## 18. Kallaway's script framework (added 2026-09-22; the user: "anything you can learn from this man is gold")

Source: Kallaway, "How To Write A Killer Script That Keeps Viewers Hooked" (`7I50PECz7SU`, Feb 2025, 606K views; transcript in `renders/transcripts/ref-7I50PECz7SU.en.txt`). The whole thing rests on one idea and five steps.

**The one idea: expectations versus reality.** The viewer arrives with an expectation set by the title and thumbnail. Every decision in the script exists to make reality beat that expectation. When it does they stay. When it doesn't they leave. "Click confirmation" is the first application: the first lines confirm the title, then add something that beats it ("unprecedented access").

**The five steps, in this order:** packaging (idea, title, loose thumbnail) → outline → intro → body → outro. Outline before intro, because the intro is a formula anyone can print and the real value is whether the body has something unique to say. If the outline is not unique, go back to research; do not make the video.

**The intro formula (works every time, his words):**
1. Immediate context. Bluntly say what the video is about in the first lines. Confirm the click.
2. State the common belief. What people already think about this. It tells them you're talking about something they care about and it sets up the contrast.
3. The contrarian take. Contradict the common belief. This is the moment reality beats expectation.
4. Proof and a plan, in either order. Why trust you, and an ordered list of what's coming.

**The body:**
- The first point after the intro has to be amazing. If it repeats what they already know they leave at point one.
- Order points by novelty with the **second-best first and the best second**, so value rises and the brain expects the pattern to continue. (For a story with a twist, the twist stays last; the ascending rule governs everything before it.)
- Every point is a **value loop**: context (what it is, simple), application (how it works, with examples), framing (why it matters and how it fits the whole story). Zoom in, zoom out, in a wave.
- **Re-hook between points.** "That point was important but if you don't also get this next one the magic is lost." Attention decays from the moment of peak interest; the re-hook resets it.

**The outro:** leave on a high note. Remind them what they learned and that the pain point got solved. It's the free chocolate with the check: it makes them remember the whole meal was good, and it's where the likes, shares and rewatches come from. (This sits beside our "no recap" rule, not against it: the recap he means is one breath, in the host's own words, framed as what you now know, not an outline read back.)

**Native embeds:** a call to action goes where a point naturally makes it the solve, never as a break in the flow.

**How the SmileDirectClub script uses it (v22):** the intro runs his four parts in order (bone dissolving and the $2,000 box confirm the click; "everybody thinks a company got greedy and got caught" is the common belief; "the plastic was fine, one sentence killed it" is the contrarian take; the credential and "three parts" are proof and plan). The body is three parts that rise: what your mouth is doing, what they skipped, what killed them. Each part has a re-hook line into the next. The outro says the three things back in one breath and lands on the high note.

## 19. Kallaway's six storytelling tips (added 2026-09-22; user: "another good one")

Source: Kallaway, "How To Become A Master Storyteller" (`t5Z-Q1bg1tU`, Nov 2024, 2.8M views; transcript in `renders/transcripts/ref-t5Z-Q1bg1tU.en.txt`).

1. **The dance.** Context, then conflict, then context, then conflict. Between every beat of the outline the word is **but** or **therefore**, never **and then** (the South Park rule, which is Olson's ABT in section 11 said by its inventors). "And then" piles detail and the viewer drifts. His Stanley Cup video has four "but" turns in the first 30 seconds. Test: read only the connectives between beats. If they are "and then", rewrite the beat as a complication.
2. **Rhythm.** Vary sentence length on purpose (the Gary Provost passage: "this sentence has five words... the ear demands some variety"). He writes every sentence on its own line and looks for a jagged right edge. If the edge is straight the script drones. This is the same finding as our blind test: uniform eight-word sentences read as performed.
3. **Tone.** The most successful creators sound like they are in the room with you. Write and film as if to one close friend; tape their photo under the lens if you have to. Same as the kitchen-table prompt.
4. **Direction.** Write the last line first and work backwards. He calls it the last dab: a last line good enough that someone who heard only that would share it. On loops (shorts) the last line sets up the first. Then write the first line, leave the middle empty, and fill in the dance.
5. **Story lens.** The topic is not the differentiator; the angle is. Taylor Swift at the Super Bowl: what she wore (common), a prediction (less common), the business impact on the NFL (his, a million views). Ours on SmileDirectClub: not the business collapse, the anatomy and the one sentence.
6. **The hook.** First line as punchy and as indicative of the plot as possible, never "wait till you see this". And a **visual hook**: the eyes perceive faster than the ears, so the first frame shows the thing before the first sentence finishes. Ours: the root with the bone going dark at 0:03.

**Applied to v22:** a "but" turn was inserted after the IPO beat ("But here's the thing nobody in that room was looking at"), which had been an "and then" sequence; one long sentence was added to the history for rhythm; the ending was rewritten to a last dab ("So before anybody starts moving stuff around in there somebody should look. That's the whole story. Somebody should look.").

## 20. The Magic School Bus bar (added 2026-09-22; the user: "something the magic school bus could pull off. A story with edutainment. Perfectly crafted for all ages with the gist of what's going on without being super technical")

The braces-face script went through seven drafts that were each correct, sourced and in voice, and each carried about 20 numbers a viewer had to hold (sample sizes, millimetres, percentages, evidence grades). The user's verdict: too much data. The bar for every explainer from now on is a Magic School Bus episode: one trip, a guide, two pictures at most, and the gist.

1. **One trip.** Pick a journey the viewer can ride from start to end and make the chapters its stops. Braces-face: the 4 years between the picture at 12 and the picture at 16. Not "part one, part two."
2. **At most two images.** One per big idea, used all the way through. Braces-face: the lips are a curtain on a curtain rod; a 12-year-old's face is a construction site. Everything else is said plainly.
3. **Six numbers or fewer on camera.** Count them (grep the spoken lines for digits). Keep only the ones a kid can hold: an age, a count of teeth, a millimetre and its coin, a year range. Every sample size, confidence figure and evidence grade moves to the facts list and the pinned comment. On camera a study is "the biggest study we've got" or "a study that followed kids for years."
4. **Honesty survives the cut.** The caveat stays but as a sentence a person would say: "the research that says don't worry about it isn't as good as we act like it is. Me included." Not "graded very low."
5. **Nothing to do.** No test for the viewer, nothing to hold up. The pencil test went.
6. **Two medical words maximum**, each said once, each explained by the sentence it's in (adenoids, sleep apnea).
7. **The dance still runs.** Read the connectives between stops: but, so, but, so. If a stop is "and then" it is a list and gets cut or turned into a complication.
8. **Last line first**, and it should be the thing a kid repeats at dinner: "Don't look at the teeth. Look at the nose."

Cost: the cut runs about 5 minutes of speech and the checker shows the short-sentence fingerprint again (avg 8.8 words, many fragments). The user's ear is the gate for this register; if it reads choppy, join sentences with "and" rather than add material. Test: hand the chapter list to someone who has never thought about teeth and ask them to tell the story back. If they can, ship.

## 21. The talking-points loop (added 2026-09-23; the user: "give me talking points, then I can speak them via a voice chat loosely back and then you create a script from that")

Nine drafts of one script in a written voice still did not sound like Ben. The fix is to stop writing his sentences. The writer supplies structure and facts; Ben supplies the sentences; the writer assembles.

1. **Talking points file** (`videos/<slug>/talking-points.md`). Top: the story in one line, the two images he may lean on, the list of numbers he is allowed to say (sourced) with "anything not on this list, leave out." Then six beats in the Magic School Bus shape (section 20). Each beat: a goal in one line, 3 to 6 bullets of substance (facts and turns, not sentences), the connective to the next beat written as BUT or SO (section 19, the dance), a "tangent welcome" hint so he adds what a clinician would add, and a "don't say" line wherever there is legal or invention risk. Bottom: ground rules (nothing invented, nothing physical, allegations attributed, skip a number rather than guess) and how to hand the recording back.
2. **He talks.** Loosely, with restarts and tangents. Phone recording is fine. Transcript via `npm run transcribe -- --in videos/<slug>/raw/<file>` or pasted.
3. **Script from transcript.** Keep his sentences and his order wherever they carry the beat. Cut restarts, keep the good tangents, move a beat only if the dance needs it. Check every number against research.md and correct it silently in the script with a note. Apply the comma rule. Add the [VISUAL] cues and the packaging. Deliver with a change list: every place a sentence is not his, and why.
4. **The test** is section 16's true test: his recorded read of the assembled script against his unscripted videos. If a paragraph gets caught, it is one the writer wrote, and it goes back to him as a talking point.

Bullets must not be readable prose. If a bullet is a sentence he could say verbatim he will say it verbatim and the voice is lost again.
