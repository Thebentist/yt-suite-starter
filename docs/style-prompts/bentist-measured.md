# TheBentist house voice (measured, 2026-09-27)

Built from 80 of Ben's own videos (`voice/thebentist/`: transcripts, fingerprint, passage bank, `voice-guide.md`, blind-test calibration). It replaces `bentist-casual.md` (Sep 22, built from 3 transcripts and an invented slang list; kept only as history). Ben, 2026-09-27: "I wasn't a fan of that writing... I trust your info over what was built from that." When this page and the transcripts disagree, the transcripts win.

## The one rule
Write every beat from his real passages, not from this page. Before each beat: `node pipeline/voice/passages.mjs query --channel thebentist --type <type> --function <job> --topic "<subject>" --n 4`. Copy how those passages move (how sentences start, how long they run before a short one lands, where the hedge sits, when he turns to "you"). Never copy their sentences. This page is the checklist for after.

## Rhythm depends on the video type
Measured from his host passages (`passages.mjs stats`):
| type | words/sentence | ≤6 / 7-14 / 15-24 / 25+ words | feel |
|---|---|---|---|
| react | 13.4 | 34 / 36 / 18 / 12 % | bursts: a reaction, a clinical read, a joke, back to the clip |
| explainer | 12.6 | 28 / 40 / 21 / 11 % | medium sentences chained with *and then*, a short verdict now and then |
| qa | 18.9 | 16 / 31 / 25 / 29 % | long run-on answers full of "you" |
| personal | 13.9 | 21 / 43 / 21 / 14 % | "I" twice as often as anywhere else |
Across all of them: about 9% questions, almost no exclamation marks, "you/your" about 42 per 1,000 words (twice other creators), "I/me/my" about 30. After a long sentence, the next one is short about 1 time in 4 (not every time: that's the scripted rhythm judges catch).

## How he does each job
- **Open.** Weekly/reaction: "What's up everybody? I'm Dr. Winters and guess what?" plus an aside about the week. Compilation: the title promise first, then the credential as a job ("I'm a dentist, I look at teeth all day"), then *let's get into it*. Explainer: a question he's about to answer (*what if I told you* is more often a mid-video pivot). Out of the intro within about three sentences.
- **Explain.** One household comparison with *almost like* / *kind of like* (termites in wood, cement, sandpaper, velcro), then name it: *this is what we call a...*. A plain *and then* chain of what happens. *fun fact* as a hinge. Land the consequence on "you" ("that bone will just dissolve on you").
- **React.** Interjection, then the clinical read ("Oh my gosh... That is a big open bite I'm seeing there"). Default verdict *that's crazy*. Verdict first on a wrong claim ("No, wrong."). Dry sarcasm (*it's almost like a retainer is supposed to retain your teeth*). Kind to the person, hard on the idea.
- **Hedge.** Likelihood, not disclaimers: *probably*, *more than likely*, *my guess is*, *I'm pretty sure*, *I don't know*. Own the gap ("It's been a long time since my human anatomy lessons"). Hand off to the specialist ("for my plastic surgeons out there, let me know down below").
- **Move on.** *first off*, *either way*, *and guess what*, *in fact*, *anyways*, *that being said*, *all right what do we got here*. End a beat on *but yeah* / *so yeah* or on an instruction ("So make sure you floss. It's really important.").
- **Lists.** One or two items, then *and stuff like that* / *things like that*. Never three tidy items.
- **Close.** Explainers ask a real question to the comments first ("I don't know, let me know down in the comments below"). Then the formula: "If you enjoyed it, you know what to do. I'll see you in the next one." (*next week* on weekly videos). Shorts stop on the advice ("So probably don't do that.").

## Words
Use his, one or two per beat, never stacked: *actually, basically, kind of, pretty, really, probably, you know, like, so, now, okay, right?, guys / you guys, dude* (reactions), *for real though* (joke to point), *straight up, legit* (rare), *I'm not gonna lie, oh my gosh, oh my goodness, holy moly*.
Don't use (never or almost never in 80 videos): *here's the thing, in other words, therefore, which means* (rare), *turns out* (rare), *incredibly, sort of* (he says *kind of*), *walk you through, let me explain* (once), *no shot, hits different, I'll be honest with you*.
Contractions by default (he contracts 78% of the time); uncontract only for stress ("It is so hard.").

## What gives a script away (from blind-test judges catching our scripts at 100%)
Never: a callback that bookends a section, a paragraph that ends on a button line or aphorism, parallel lists ("It's the office. It's the staff..."), a punchy analogy followed by aphoristic short sentences, a curiosity-gap line with a reveal ("here's what most people don't know... It was in your mouth"), stacked explanatory fragments ("Which means the laws. The nine states."), signposting or roadmaps, recaps, thesis/antithesis ("It's not X. It's Y."), sincerity tags, a joke placed on its own line. What real Ben has instead: a self-correction mid-sentence, a clause that trails off, the same point said twice in different words, a meandering hedge, a tangent he comes back from.

**"Too orderly" is the main tell** (wisdom-teeth proof run, round 1: Sonnet judges 83% and 75%, even with voice-check at 99/100 and the right words and cadence). What they caught: clean cause→effect chains ("Now... But... So that's why... And that's why"), a metaphor extended with perfect consistency, tidy do/don't advice sequences, a neatly balanced concession ("it wasn't perfect... but it's still the best we've got"), an inserted gloss for the audience ("which just means..."), a reaction placed on its own line before a punchline, a set-up joke, rhetorical turn questions ("So what actually stops it?"), punchy standalone fragments ("Because you're not done changing."), paragraphs that end on a clean hand-off back to the thesis. Write in his order of thought instead: let an explanation wander and come back, restart a sentence with a correction ("they got, well basically they got..."), half-drop a comparison, put the reaction inside the sentence. It must still read aloud naturally: never fake stutters, repeated words or filler sounds; he adds his own when he talks.

**After that, the summary line is the last tell** (round 2: judges down to 58% and 63%, every catch at low confidence). Paragraph-closing summaries ("So those are real reasons.", "And people believe that.", "So no wonder they get blamed.") and long evenly paced explanations with no aside still get caught. End a paragraph mid-thought, on a hedge, an aside or just the last fact, and put one of his asides (an opinion, a reaction inside a sentence, a tangent he comes back from, a "you guys" check-in) inside any explanation longer than about 60 words. Mention research loosely, the way he does, with a hedge on the detail.

**Round 3 tells** (judges at 67% and 54%, every catch at the lowest confidence): an outro that stacks calls to action (retainer reminder, then comment prompt, then subscribe) instead of his loose formula; objection-acknowledgment phrases ("And that's a fair question", "You might be thinking fine, but..."); outline-like transitions ("Well a couple things. And the first one..."); a closing directive written to be quotable ("Go find your retainer and put it in tonight"). Sweep the whole script for these, not just the paragraphs a judge happened to sample.

**Exposés and anything with legal attribution** (kids-dental-chain script, round 1: judges 91% and 81%). Legal care means every allegation is attributed, and a tag sentence ("That's from the American Dental Association.", "That's your right under HIPAA.") or an itemized list of the settlement's claims reads as a prepared summary. Keep the attribution, say it his way, inside the sentence and loosely ("and the ADA actually looked at this, it's like 1 in 3", "and I think NBC was the one that reported..."); give lists as one or two items plus "and stuff like that" where the record allows a paraphrase; keep exact wording only where `legal-review.md` says it matters. He reacts to a reported quote the way he reacts to a clip: quote the line, then react inside the sentence.

## Punctuation
Write for reading aloud. Numbers as digits ($5,000, 2023) and dates inside a clause ("so then in December of 2023 they..."). Join clauses the way he does, with *and / so / because / which*, rather than chopping them into fragments. No exclamation marks.

## Signature lines and bits
At most once per video: *What's up everybody I'm Dr. Winters*, "the internet's dentist", "Oh my goodness gracious", "edumacate", "what do I know? I'm just a dentist", the outro formula. The 10-million bracket bit and his toothpaste only when Ben asks for them.

## Still true from Ben's rules (docs/ben-rules.md)
Talking head only, nothing invented about Ben, never naive about a fad, the Magic School Bus bar, no phantom promises, ask what a clinician would say is missing.

**Borrowed structures (Cleo's in his voice, kids-dental-chain, 2026-09-28: judges 59% and 69%).** Her signature devices are what get caught when they're delivered neatly: a triple question stack capped by "that's what we're gonna figure out", setup → rhetorical question → answer ("Would you be okay with that? Probably not"), a complete steelman then rebuttal, and clipped news-style timeline sentences. Keep the move, change the delivery: two questions wondered aloud with a hedge, the decide-first as him thinking it through, the skeptic said loosely ("to be fair... I don't know") with a restart, the timeline in his run-ons.

## When he reads a script (learned from the bad-breath read, 2026-09-28)
Measured from his 32-minute take of `videos/bad-breath-for-good/script.md`: 158 script sentences aligned to what he said. Evidence and timestamps are in `videos/bad-breath-for-good/take/read-lessons.md`. In the blind read test the judges scored 50% and 53% (chance). It was marked FAIL only because both caught one paragraph, and what they flagged there were his own ad-libs ("microscopically", "weirdly enough"). So this is about making the copy easier to say, not about sounding more like him.
- **He memorizes about 35 words per glance** (2-3 short sentences) and says them his way. Sentences of 10 words or fewer came out near-verbatim (match 0.72). Over 30 words, 64% were rebuilt or lost. Keep spoken sentences under about 20 words, one idea each, with the subject and verb together. A garden-path line ("people who were worried about their breath rate their own breath") cost a 6-second stall and a restart.
- **Don't pre-place his hinges.** He adds them himself: *guess what* (0 in the script, 5 said), *fun fact* (0 → 4), *you see* (0 → 6), *trust me* (1 → 3), *to be honest* (0 → 3), *you know* (2 → 25). Write at most one per paragraph. The flavor words we placed (*dude*, *I'm not gonna lie*) were dropped.
- **He voices the trip's turns.** Examples: "So this leads us to the back of the tongue", "let's keep going back into the throat now", "now that we know where bad breath comes from what next?". He also said our headings aloud. Write each `###` heading as the turn he'd say, and keep transitions and roadmaps out of the lines.
- **Studies: plain and nameless.** He says "There was actually a study that looked at these on CT scans", not "I think there was a study". He dropped the named experts and institutions (Cleveland Clinic, Dr. James Burns at Northwestern) and botched the one name he tried ("yeah I completely botched that"). Put names in the pinned comment. Hedge the size of a claim (*more than likely* survived 2 of 2), not whether a study exists.
- **Numbers: one per sentence, one qualifier at most.** Simple ratios and counts survived: 8 or 9 out of 10, 1 in 3, 2,000, 10 seconds, 2 weeks. Qualified ones bent: "less than half" became "almost always got it wrong... like half", and "1 in 4 on CT scans" became "25% of the world". Hedged ranges ("a week or so, maybe 2") and "in those shorter studies" were dropped.
- **Images: give it once, plainly, and he builds on it.** The shag carpet grew "all the shag moving around"; he coined "saliva is basically the power washer of your mouth". After an analogy he says the point out loud ("It's the same thing with your mouth"), so don't write that line for him. He dropped the image callback in our close.
- **Examples and list closers are his.** "the garlic and all that type of stuff", "sometimes even black if it gets really crazy". Leave room for them rather than writing them.
- **Advice lists and caveats get dropped.** A paragraph of 7 instructions came out as 2 sentences, and 7 steps of tonsil-stone care came out as 2 (lowest setting, gargle after). Give lists as beats (substance bullets) and keep only the 2-3 he must say.
- **Where he disagrees, he says his view.** Our "Maybe... I wouldn't lose sleep over it" on alcohol mouthwash became "I definitely wouldn't use alcohol mouthwash if I was you". Any line that softens a common dental recommendation is a clinician's-point question for him before he records (ben-rules 4), not a line to slip in.
- **His rhythm when reading is slower than when talking.** Script content ran at 188 wpm after silence cuts, against 230 in his own riffs. He also said 1.34 words per script word. Time scripts at 140 script words per finished minute (`channels/thebentist.json` `script_wpm`), not his 230 talking pace.
