## 1. The test a script must pass
Read any paragraph out loud at speed. If it sounds like an essay, a trailer, or a TED talk, it fails. Ben does not deliver sentences. He talks. Cleo does not write aphorisms. She tells you what she found out.

Ben's actual sentences:
> "What if I told you that losing teeth is no longer permanent? Because today we find out if regrowing teeth is real or if it's just internet hype."
> "One in five Americans over 75 years old have lost all of their teeth. That is insane."
> "I know that was really long, but basically think of it as a brake pedal for tooth formation."
> "And I'm not going to lie, I was surprised. I always thought that you just had those two tooth buds and that was it."
> "It's actually an Alzheimer drug called Tideglusib. I could be totally botching that, but it triggers stem cells inside your tooth."
> "So let's just keep everybody grounded here, okay?"
> "Maybe you put it there and you end up growing a tooth through your chin. We just don't know yet."
> "But what if I told you that I've seen teeth that look like this not because of Dr. Pepper, but because of milk. Yeah, that's right, milk."

Cleo's actual sentences:
> "This... is... Antarctica! And in 1985, it became the center of a global crisis."
> "And if we hadn't done anything about it, today we'd be living in an alternate world. By 2040, that hole would be over the entire planet."
> "I remember hearing all about the ozone hole as a kid. And then one day the news about it just stopped... and I stopped thinking about it."

> "Well, there goes my home."

## 2. Ben's voice, specifically
- **Spoken grammar.** Sentences start with So, Now, And, But, Because, Okay. Run-ons are fine. Fragments are fine. "Yeah, that's right, milk."
- **His words:** actually, basically, kind of, crazy, insane, honestly, listen, dude, you guys, guess what, okay?, let me explain, you see, here's the thing, not gonna lie, what if I told you, it's a real big deal, keep everybody grounded. Use them the way he does: as connective tissue, two or three per paragraph, not as a costume.
- **He reacts to his own facts.** Stat, then a one-line reaction: "That is insane." "It's kind of insane when you really think about it." "Which is kind of crazy, but also makes sense if you think about sharks."
- **He explains with one everyday object.** Brake pedal. Shark. Varnish you paint on. Baby bottle. Never two metaphors for one idea.
- **He admits uncertainty on camera** and it makes him more credible, not less: "I could be totally botching that." "We just don't know yet." "I was surprised."
- **He is kind to the person, hard on the idea.** "Luckily, this guy is just an actor." "I'm not going to cancel you, but what you're doing is kind of cancelling out how those rubber bands work. Let me explain."
- **Clinic authority is casual, not ceremonial.** "It just leaves my patients wishing one thing." "I've seen teeth that look like this." Never "I move faces for a living, on purpose, with a written plan."
- **He has a company.** Something Nice / Zero Pro toothpaste; he mentions it naturally when relevant ("even my company is working on this"). Leave a slot for it when the topic touches bacteria or cavities; do not force it.
- **Openers he actually uses:** "What's up everybody? I'm Dr. Ben." (react videos) and "What if I told you..." (explainers). Closers: "keep flossing," "ask your orthodontist," a specific next video.
- **Humor is a specific image, not a quip.** "Growing a tooth through your chin." "Are you good for a year from now for your next cleaning?" No punchlines built on wordplay.

## 3. Cleo's build, specifically (what we borrow)
1. **Start inside a scene, present tense, with an object or place.** "I'm scaling a glacier right now." For Ben: he is in the clinic, holding the thing, or watching the video, right now.
2. **News clips or viral clips as receipts** in the first minute, three or four quick quotes, no commentary yet.
3. **Counterfactual stakes.** "If we hadn't done anything about it..." with dated steps. Ben's version: "if this were true, here is what your mouth would look like in 20 years."
4. **Personal memory.** "I remember hearing about this as a kid." Ben's: "I remember the first patient who asked me this."
5. **A journey with real friction and small jokes.** She is nervous on the plane, the wind pushes her back, "there goes my home." Ben's: the awkward call to a colleague, the paper he could not understand, the thing he got wrong.
6. **A person with a question at the centre of the history.** "A 30-year-old scientist with a question." "A dinner party on April 5th, 1950." Dates, names, a room.
7. **"I'm paraphrasing here a lot."** She flags simplification instead of pretending precision.
8. **The recurring line.** "Chose a different future" three times, then "the next big choice" at the end. One line, repeated, closes the loop.
9. **The sponsor sits at about one third**, in her own voice, connected to the trip.
10. **No aphorisms, no triplets, no taglines.** Her surprise comes from a fact at scale ("91% of the world's fresh water"), never from phrasing.

## 4. Hard rules for our scripts
- **Word budget: 2,300-3,000 spoken words** for an 11-13 minute explainer. Ben's measured pace is about 225 words a minute (regrow-teeth video: 2,497 words in 11:03; soda video: 3,256 in 12:37 including clips), not the 155 wpm generic estimate. Time every chapter at 225 wpm. The first drafts felt padded not because of the count but because of the cue density and the essay sentences.
- **One visual note per 60-120 words**, written as `[VISUAL: what we see]`, and only where a picture does real work. Animations are built later from the recorded transcript, so the script is not a shot list. The first drafts had a cue on every sentence; that is the single biggest reason they read as fake.
- **No tagline sign-offs.** Not "I'm Ben. I move teeth for a living. Let's find out what moves with them." He would say: "So let's get into it."
- **No stacked triplets** ("There are two big studies... There is a thing your face does... And there is one decision...") unless it is a real list he is going to walk through immediately.
- **No trailer promises.** Cleo says "let me show you how that happened," once, and then shows you. Do not preview every chapter before the first chapter.
- **No stage directions about attitude** ("leaning in, not smug"). Ben decides how he feels.
- **No sentence Ben would have to rehearse.** If it has a semicolon, a colon, a dash-aside, or a subordinate clause before the main verb, rewrite it as two spoken sentences.
- **Numbers spoken the way he says them:** "one in five," "two million," "four millimeters, which is about the width of a pencil eraser."
- **Chapter questions stay verbatim** (search still matters), but he asks them the way he would: "Okay, so do braces actually change your face shape?"
- **Kind to people, hard on claims**, always. The channel rule, in his cadence.

## 5. Before and after (the cold open of the braces-and-face draft)
**Before (draft 1):**
> This is a profile, and this is the question that will not die: do braces change your face shape? A forehead, a nose, a top lip, a bottom lip, a chin, and hidden under that lip, the reason a lot of you are scared of braces. The answer is yes. A little. In one third of it. [six cues] I'm an orthodontist. I move faces for a living, on purpose, with a written plan.

**After (voice guide):**
> What if I told you that braces can change your face? Because they can. A little. And this week two million people watched a thirty-minute video that says it's way worse than a little. That braces break your face. That they age you. That American orthodontists are doing it on purpose.
> [VISUAL: the claim, no name, no face]
> Now, I'm an orthodontist. Moving teeth around under people's faces is literally my whole job. And I'm not going to sit here and tell you that video is all wrong, because honestly, about half of it isn't. So today we're going to find out exactly what braces do to your face, what they don't, and the one decision that gets made when you're about eleven years old that matters more than any of it. Let's get into it.

Same facts. Fewer words. He can say it without reading it.
