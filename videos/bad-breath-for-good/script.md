## Script

### Open

[VISUAL: animated, close two-shot: two people talking, one of them leans back about an inch mid-sentence; freeze on the lean, and the thumbnail's tongue (back third circled in red) pops up in a thought bubble over the speaker's head]

So you're talking to somebody, like close enough to see their teeth, and right in the middle of your sentence they lean back a little. And now you're sitting there going, wait, do I have bad breath right now? And if I do, how do I get rid of it for good, because would anybody even tell me?

[VISUAL: the thought-bubble tongue grows to fill the frame: big, the back third circled in red and slightly yellow-green]

And the crazy part is you can't really check. Because you're actually pretty bad at smelling your own breath. Everybody is. It's kind of like when you walk into somebody else's house and it's got a smell, and yours doesn't, well, not to you anyway.

And most people think bad breath is coming from their stomach or something they ate. But I'm an orthodontist, I'm in people's mouths all day, so trust me, I know what breath smells like, and more than likely it's actually your mouth, like 8 or 9 out of 10 times it's the mouth. And it's super common. About 1 in 3 people have it, so if that's you, you are definitely not alone.

So how do you even check something you can't smell? And where is it really coming from? Because I don't think it's where most people think it is, so I kind of want to go through the whole thing, from the tip of your tongue all the way down.

### Why you can't smell your own breath

So first off, the reason you can't smell your own breath is your nose basically gets used to anything that's around you all the time. It just kind of stops noticing it. And there's nothing around you more than your own breath, I mean it's coming out of your face all day. And the whole cup your hand over your mouth and sniff thing, that doesn't really work either. You're just smelling the same air you've been smelling all day, and I think Cleveland Clinic even says it's tough to tell that way.

And people have actually tested how good we are at this. They had people who were worried about their breath rate their own breath, and then somebody trained to smell breath, like that's literally their job, rated it too. And the people only got it right less than half the time.

So there's this thing you can do. It's kind of gross. You lick your wrist, like the inside of it, and you wait like 10 seconds for it to dry, and then you smell it. So go ahead and actually do it right now. I'll wait.

[VISUAL: a big on-screen counter, 10 to 0, over a simple animated wrist; hold the full 10 seconds, no talking, a soft tick each second]

Okay. So if that smelled bad, that's probably what people are getting when you talk to them. Sorry. And if it didn't, well, that doesn't totally mean you're in the clear. Because your wrist is really only getting the tip of your tongue, and the tip is actually the cleanest part of your whole tongue. It's rubbing on the roof of your mouth, your spit's washing over it all day long, so it's pretty clean up there. It's the back that's the problem. That is why when a dentist checks this for real, they go to the back of your tongue and scrape a little off and smell that.

### The back of your tongue

[VISUAL: the trip starts at the tip of the tongue and moves back across it; the little bumps get longer and thicker toward the back, like a shag carpet, with crumbs and gunk settling down between the fibers]

So the back of your tongue. If you look at a tongue up close, it's not smooth at all. It's covered in these little tiny bumps, and as you go further back they get longer and longer. It's almost like a shag carpet, I mean like the old ones. And everything gets stuck down in there, little bits of food and mucus and dead cells from the tongue and the cheeks. And spit can't really get down in there to wash it out.

And there are bacteria that absolutely love it down there. They're the kind that actually don't like oxygen, and down in that carpet there's not a lot of air. So I mean it's kind of the perfect little spot for them. And they eat all that gunk, and a lot of that gunk is basically protein, the dead cells and the mucus and stuff. And some of the stuff protein is made out of has sulfur in it. So when the bacteria break it down, well, they basically poop out that sulfur as a gas. We call them volatile sulfur compounds. And one of the main ones is the same gas that makes rotten eggs smell like rotten eggs.

[VISUAL: little green puffs rise out of the carpet; a cracked egg drifts in beside them for a beat]

And it's kind of crazy when you think about it. What you're smelling is basically bacteria digesting dead cells off the back of your tongue. And you can actually see it sometimes. If you stick your tongue out in the mirror and the back is kind of white or yellowish, that is what we call a coated tongue, and that's probably where yours is coming from.

And you'd think it'd be your teeth, right? That's what you've been brushing your whole life. But there's this bad breath clinic over in Belgium, a periodontist named Marc Quirynen and his team, and I'm probably saying that wrong. It's like a whole clinic just for breath. And they went through like 2,000 patients, and the number one cause by a lot was just that coating on the tongue. Way more than gum problems or anything with their teeth. And a good chunk of the people who came in didn't even have bad breath, they were just really worried that they did.

### Down to the tonsils

[VISUAL: the trip pulls out to the whole mouth: food wedged between two back teeth, a pink gum line going red; then a slow stream of saliva running over everything, drying to a trickle at night]

Now the other spots in your mouth are kind of the same idea. In between your teeth, food gets stuck and it just sits there, and the same kind of bacteria get to it, and if that goes on long enough, well, your gums start getting infected and eventually the bone around your teeth starts going too. That's gum disease. And those gum disease bacteria make the same sulfur smell, so bad breath that just won't go away can actually be a sign of gum disease. And if you've got braces, dude, it's even worse. There's so many more little spots around the brackets for food to hide.

And your spit is a big part of all this. Saliva is basically what's washing everything out all day, and when you're asleep it slows way down. I think that's pretty much why you wake up with morning breath. And if you sleep with your mouth open, well, now it's drying out even more. And there are literally hundreds of medicines that can dry your mouth out too, so if you're on one of those, that could be part of it.

[VISUAL: the trip keeps going over the back of the tongue and down to the tonsils: little pits in the tonsil, a crumb gets squeezed into a white pebble with each swallow]

Now if you keep going back, past your tongue, you get to your tonsils. And your tonsils have these little holes in them, we call them crypts. Food and gunk get stuck up in there, and every time you swallow it gets squished down and down until it's this little stone of, basically, rotting food. That's a tonsil stone. And they smell different, like a whole different kind of bad. It's nasty. If you've ever squished one, you know exactly what I'm talking about.

And plenty of people have them and have no idea. I think there was a study that looked at CT scans, and they showed up in about 1 in 4 people. But as far as bad breath goes, they're actually a pretty small slice of it overall, even though they're the ones that freak everybody out.

### Isn't it your stomach?

[VISUAL: the trip looks on down the throat toward the stomach; a little valve at the top of the stomach, shut]

So if you keep going, past the tonsils and down the throat, that's kind of where everybody's head goes. Like isn't bad breath coming from my stomach? Well, sometimes. Reflux can do it. Sinus stuff can do it too, because all that drainage from your nose drips right down onto the back of your tongue. And with some medical things like really bad diabetes, you can actually smell it on somebody's breath. It smells kind of sweet, which is weird.

But your stomach isn't just breathing out of your mouth all day. There's a little valve at the top of it, well, technically the bottom of your esophagus, and it stays shut unless you're swallowing or burping, or you've got reflux and it's not closing right. So for most people it really is the mouth. How much is everything else, I don't know, the studies kind of go back and forth on that one. So for my doctors out there, let me know down below.

### Why nothing you've tried has worked

So if you've had bad breath for a while, you've probably tried stuff. Mints and mouthwash, maybe brushing harder. And the reason none of it really sticks is kind of all the same reason. A mint just covers it up for a few minutes, and then the smell's right back, because the bacteria are still sitting back there making it, and if it's a sugary mint, you're feeding your cavity bacteria while you're at it. And most mouthwash, I mean, it's pretty temporary too, for the same reason. And brushing harder, I mean, you're brushing your teeth, and it's back on your tongue the whole time.

So I think people kind of have this backwards. They think bad breath means you're dirty or you're not trying. And more than likely you're doing exactly what you got told, brushing and flossing and all that. It's just that the spot that's actually making it is the one spot most people never really clean.

### The honest part

Now to be fair, I don't want to oversell this. The research on fixing bad breath is honestly not great. It's a lot of small studies, and there's a big review that went through a bunch of them and basically said the evidence for pretty much everything is weak. And for the tongue scraper stuff specifically, the studies say a scraper does a little bit better than a toothbrush, but not by a ton. And it wears off pretty quick, the coating just builds right back up, which is why it has to be an every day thing.

And the alcohol mouthwash thing, people always say it dries your mouth out and makes it worse. Maybe. I think there was actually a trial where they had people use either one for a week, and they didn't really feel any drier. So I'd still skip the alcohol if your mouth's already dry, but I wouldn't lose sleep over it. So I'm not saying cleaning your tongue is some miracle thing, but it's cheap and it's safe, and in those shorter studies people's breath did get better in like 2 weeks, so yeah, I'd still do it.

### What actually works

[VISUAL: back on the shag-carpet tongue: a U-shaped scraper sets down in the middle and pulls forward, clearing a stripe; then a toothbrush does the same, a little less clean]

So if you came in and asked me, I'd tell you the first thing is clean your tongue every day. A scraper or your toothbrush, whichever one you're actually going to do. You want to go as far back as you can, set it down gently and pull it forward, rinse it, and do that a few times. And I'm not gonna lie, the first couple times you'll probably gag. And people are like, I'm never doing that again, and no, just start a little further forward and breathe through your nose. And go a little further back every day, and your gag kind of gets used to it.

And then clean in between your teeth once a day. Floss, water flosser, whatever you'll actually stick with. And get the back side of your very last tooth too, because that's the one people miss. And for the dry mouth stuff, drink water during the day, and chew some sugar free gum after you eat. Xylitol gum is a good one, but honestly it's more the chewing, because chewing is what gets your spit going. If you sleep with your mouth open, bring that up with your dentist or your doctor. And if it's a medicine drying you out, talk to whoever prescribed it. Do not just stop taking it on your own.

### The tonsil stones

[VISUAL: the tonsil from before, lit like a flashlight is on it: two little white spots in the pits; then a gentle, slow stream comes in from the side and rinses one out]

Now the tonsil stones. If you want to know if you have them, you can look back there with a flashlight and see if there's little white or yellow spots on your tonsils. Or you'll get that feeling like something's scratching the back of your throat and you can't quite get it down, and that's normally one about to come out. And please don't go digging at them with a cotton swab or your finger. Your tonsils bleed really easily and they can get infected. There's an ear, nose and throat doctor at Northwestern, Dr. James Burns, who basically says don't scrape at them at home at all. So warm salt water gargles are a good place to start.

And if you want to flush them out with a water flosser, a regular one is really made to blast stuff off teeth, so it's way too strong for the back of your throat. It's gonna hurt. Which is actually why at my company Something Nice we made ours with a slower soft mode for back there [BEN: optional, the only product mention in the video: keep or cut this sentence; confirm the wording]. But with any of them, lowest setting, come at it from the side, and if it hurts or it bleeds, you stop. And if they're really big or they keep coming back, that's ear, nose and throat stuff. They can take them out in the office, and if it's really bad they can laser those little holes shut or take the tonsils out, which I know is a little intense, but it's an option. And once they're out, gargling at night helps keep those holes clean so new ones don't form.

### For good

[VISUAL: a strip of 14 days across the bottom of the frame; the coating on the animated tongue fades a little each day]

Now for good, I mean, those bacteria are going to be back in your mouth tomorrow no matter what you do. There's no one time fix. For good just kind of means it's part of what you do every day, like brushing. And for most people, if it's the tongue or the gums, I'd say you'll probably notice pretty quick, like a week or so, maybe 2, it kind of depends how bad it was. But if you've been doing all of it for 2 weeks and nothing's changed, then it's probably not a hygiene thing. So go see your dentist first so they can check your gums and stuff. And if your mouth looks good, then it's your doctor, for reflux or sinus stuff or whatever it might be.

So yeah, I mean, you probably can't tell by smelling it yourself. It's probably that shag carpet on the back of your tongue, and getting rid of it is honestly just cleaning back there every day, which is kind of wild that it's that simple. And it's not just you, like literally everybody has these bacteria, everybody wakes up with morning breath, it's just that the spot making it is one nobody really thinks to clean. And I kind of love that, because it means a lot of you can probably fix this in a couple weeks, with a scraper that costs a few bucks or just the toothbrush you already have. So do the wrist thing again in a couple weeks, or just ask somebody you trust, like actually ask them, because they're probably not gonna bring it up on their own.

Would you tell a friend if their breath stunk? I don't know, let me know down in the comments below. If you enjoyed it, you know what to do. I'll see you in the next one.
