## Round 2 claim wording (new or changed on camera)
- "They're the kind that actually don't like oxygen": the sulfur-makers are anaerobes, favored by the low-oxygen tongue crevices (rows 8, 10).
- "a lot of that gunk is basically protein... some of the stuff protein is made out of has sulfur in it... they basically poop out that sulfur as a gas": sulfur-containing amino acids (cysteine, methionine) broken down into volatile sulfur compounds (row 8).
- "what you're smelling is basically bacteria digesting dead cells off the back of your tongue": tongue coating = desquamated cells, food, bacteria (rows 2, 10); a loose summary, not a measured share.
- "eventually the bone around your teeth starts going too": gum disease → bone loss (ADA row 20; Ben on camera, `RVXQmWSSfAo`, "the main reason is for gum disease and bone loss").
- "it wears off pretty quick, the coating just builds right back up": effects under 30 minutes in the Cochrane trials (row 11); "builds back up" is the plain-language reason, flagged as interpretation.
- "a big review that went through a bunch of them and basically said the evidence for pretty much everything is weak": Cochrane 2019, 44 trials, low to very-low certainty (row 11).
- "in those shorter studies people's breath did get better in like 2 weeks": rows 21 (2-week tongue-cleaning trial) and 11 (Slot 2015, 2+ week trials).
- "It's rubbing on the roof of your mouth, your spit's washing over it": the front of the tongue is relatively self-cleansing (row 7); the mechanism is the plain-language gloss.

## Facts, with sources
1. **It's common.** A systematic review and meta-regression of 13 studies put the pooled prevalence of halitosis at 31.8% (95% CI 24.6-39.0). Silva et al., Clinical Oral Investigations 2018. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28676903/ → "about 1 in 3 people".
2. **Mostly the mouth.** "Eighty to ninety percent of halitosis is caused by intra-oral factors, with coated tongue, periodontal diseases and poor oral hygiene practices being the principal factors"; 10-20% extra-oral. Memon et al., "Aetiology and associations of halitosis: a systematic review", Oral Diseases 2023. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/35212093/ . Same 80-90% in the 2026 Chinese clinical practice guideline (Zhou, Ge et al., International Dental Journal): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC12914797/ . Caveat: a periodontist's trade-press column puts the gut at 20-30% (Froum, Perio-Implant Advisory, https://www.perioimplantadvisory.com/clinical-tips/article/14285416/bad-breath-is-it-coming-from-your-gut); the clinic data below says 4%. So the script says the studies "go back and forth" on the size of the non-mouth slice.
3. **The Leuven clinic (Marc Quirynen's group).** 2,000 consecutive patients at a multidisciplinary bad-breath clinic, Department of Periodontology, Catholic University of Leuven, Belgium: 76% had an oral cause (tongue coating alone 43%, gingivitis/periodontitis 11%, both 18%); 16% pseudo-halitosis/halitophobia (no bad breath a judge could detect); ENT/extra-oral 4%; most came without referral and had complained for a mean of 7 years. Quirynen et al., J Clin Periodontol 2009;36:970-975. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19811581/ ; institution: https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/abs/10.1111/j.1600-051X.2009.01478.x → on camera (round 2): "a periodontist named Marc Quirynen and his team", "like 2,000 patients", "the number one cause by a lot was just that coating on the tongue, way more than gum problems or anything with their teeth", "a good chunk... didn't even have bad breath". The 7 years is off camera from round 2. Don't say they "brushed and tried everything" (not reported).
4. **You're bad at judging your own breath.** 180 patients complaining of bad breath self-rated and were rated by a trained examiner: 93.9% had halitosis on the organoleptic test, and self-rating matched the examiner in only 37.8%; agreement was best at mild-to-moderate levels. Romano et al., Int J Dent Hyg 2010. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20096081/ (abstract via Europe PMC) → "right less than half the time". Other studies also report weak links between self-perception and measured breath (search summary of PMC5457280 and PMC11651456).
5. **Why: nose-blindness (olfactory adaptation).** Prolonged or repeated exposure to an odor lowers perception of it, at the nose and in the brain. Human olfactory epithelium recordings: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9130158/ ; overview: https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olfactory_fatigue . Applying it to your own breath is the standard explanation (e.g. https://www.kelnerperio.com/blog/2024/3/9/why-cant-we-tell-when-our-breath-smells), not something a study measured directly. Say it loosely.
6. **Cupping your hand doesn't work.** "It's tough to tell if you have bad breath just by cupping a hand over your nose and mouth and taking a quick whiff"; asking someone you trust is suggested. Cleveland Clinic, Halitosis. https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/17771-bad-breath-halitosis
7. **The wrist test is a quick check, not a validated test.** Lick the wrist, wait 5-10 seconds to dry, smell; it mainly samples the front of the tongue, which is relatively self-cleansing, so it may under-report. https://www.animated-teeth.com/bad_breath/t1_halitosis.htm . Mayo Clinic: "Because the back of the tongue most often causes the smell, your dentist also may scrape it and rate its odor." https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/bad-breath/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20350925 *(search snippet; page returned 403)*. The organoleptic "sniff test" by a trained judge is the gold standard (2026 guideline, row 2).
8. **What the smell is.** Anaerobic, mostly gram-negative bacteria in the tongue coating and gum pockets break down sulfur-containing amino acids (cysteine, methionine) from proteins into volatile sulfur compounds; hydrogen sulfide and methyl mercaptan make up about 90% of them. Wåler 1997, Eur J Oral Sci: https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/9395120/ ; Frontiers in Oral Health 2023 narrative review: https://www.frontiersin.org/journals/oral-health/articles/10.3389/froh.2023.1229145/full (names P. gingivalis, F. nucleatum, P. intermedia, T. denticola).
9. **Rotten eggs.** Hydrogen sulfide is the smell of rotten eggs; methyl mercaptan is rotting cabbage. Compound Interest: https://www.compoundchem.com/2014/04/07/the-chemistry-of-body-odours-sweat-halitosis-flatulence-cheesy-feet/ → image 2 ("the same gas that makes rotten eggs smell").
10. **Why the back of the tongue (image 1, the shag carpet).** The tongue's top is covered in papillae; debris, cells and bacteria are trapped in the crevices between them, especially on the back third, protected from saliva's flushing and low in oxygen, which favors anaerobes; papilla depth tracked visible coating. Bernardi, Marzo, Continenza, Acta Stomatol Croat 2016. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5080560/ . Tongue coating + low saliva flow explained 54% of the variation in sulfur compounds among 92 students with morning breath (cross-sectional, 2025): https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC12429643/
11. **Scraper vs brush: modest.** Cochrane (Outhouse et al. 2006): 2 trials, 40 people; scrapers/cleaners reduced sulfur compounds slightly more than a toothbrush (42% / 40% vs 33% in one; 75% vs 45% in the other) but effects lasted under 30 minutes; "weak and unreliable evidence". https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/16625641/ . Van der Sleen 2010: tongue cleaning reduces odour and coating, but evidence in real halitosis patients is weak. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/20961381/ . Slot 2015 (≥2-week trials): the effect of a special toothpaste, mouthwash or tongue scraper "is, in general, unclear"; CHX+CPC+zinc rinses best supported, weak recommendation. https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1111/jcpe.12378 . Cochrane 2019 (44 trials, 1,809 people): low to very-low certainty for every intervention. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6905014/ . The 2026 guideline does recommend scrapers over toothbrushes (row 2). → Say "a little better, doesn't last long, do it daily"; never "scrape, don't brush".
12. **Gag tips (practical, not evidence-graded).** Start mid-tongue and pull forward, nasal breathing, go 1-2 mm further back each day over 2-3 weeks. https://oralhealthhq.com/dental-tools/how-to-use-tongue-scraper/ , https://www.wdfdental.com/blog/how-to-clean-your-tongue-without-gagging/ . Flagged for Ben (question 2).
13. **Alcohol mouthwash and dry mouth: mixed.** RCT, 163 completers, alcohol vs alcohol-free rinse twice daily for 7 days: no significant difference in Xerostomia Inventory scores; "Short-term use of alcohol-containing mouthrinse does not result in worsening xerostomia." Nair et al., J Oral Rehabil 2018. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29164661/ . Against: Mayo Clinic dry-mouth self-care, "Do not use mouthwashes that contain alcohol because they can dry your mouth." https://www.mayoclinic.org/diseases-conditions/dry-mouth/diagnosis-treatment/drc-20356052 *(search snippet)*. ADA: mouthwash is a temporary measure. https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/bad-breath
14. **Mints and gum mostly mask, briefly.** A sucrose gum lowered sulfur compounds for about 5 minutes (study summarized in search: https://www.sciencedirect.com/science/article/abs/pii/S1532338217301628). Systematic review of chewing gum (Muniz et al., J Breath Res 2017): some active gums helped, few and heterogeneous studies. https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/28212110/ . Sugar feeds the acid-making cavity bacteria (Ben's own S. mutans explanation, `_Y8IIXbfGA0`). **The brief's "sugary mints feed the bacteria" is overstated for breath:** the sulfur-makers break down proteins, not sugar (row 8). Script: a mint covers it for a few minutes, and a sugary one feeds your cavity bacteria while you're at it.
15. **Saliva, sleep, mouth breathing.** ADA: dry mouth "can be caused by certain medications, salivary gland problems or by simply breathing through your mouth"; sugar-free gum stimulates saliva. https://www.mouthhealthy.org/all-topics-a-z/bad-breath . Saliva flow drops during sleep (morning breath); lower flow tracked with more sulfur compounds (row 10, PMC12429643).
16. **Medicines.** "Hundreds of medicines can cause the salivary glands to make less saliva" (blood pressure, depression, bladder control named); sugarless gum or candy, water, a humidifier at night; see your dentist or doctor to find out why. NIDCR: https://www.nidcr.nih.gov/health-info/dry-mouth . Mayo suggests xylitol products for dry mouth (row 13 page). Don't tell anyone to stop a medicine.
17. **Tonsil stones are common, but a small slice of bad breath.** CT prevalence 24.6% of 150 (https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3699974), 39.9% of 2,873 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC4214167/), 46.1% of 482 (https://www.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/pmc/articles/PMC3852777/); most are small and silent. Tonsillar causes are reported in as few as 3% of objective halitosis complaints (Ferguson, Aydin, Mickel, Otolaryngol Head Neck Surg 2014: https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/abs/10.1177/0194599814544881), but a tonsillolith raises the risk of abnormal breath readings about tenfold (Rio et al., Br Dent J 2008: https://www.nature.com/articles/bdj.2007.1106). → "about 1 in 4 people in one CT study", "a pretty small slice of bad breath overall".
18. **Tonsil stones: checking and removal.** Symptoms include bad breath, bad taste, feeling of something stuck. Cleveland Clinic home options: warm salt-water gargle, cough, water pick, "use a cotton swab to gently push the tonsil stones out"; see a provider if they don't come out or keep coming back; tonsillectomy for persistent cases. https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/diseases/21505-tonsil-stones . Northwestern Medicine laryngologist **Dr. James A. Burns** cautions against "scraping them with your finger, a cotton swab or other tool" because "your tonsils are delicate and prone to bleeding and infection"; gargle warm salt water or mild antiseptic mouthwash; see a professional if large or uncomfortable. https://www.nm.org/healthbeat/healthy-tips/tonsil-stones-101 . Low-pressure irrigation advice ("lowest setting... aimed from the side") is from consumer guides: https://www.newmouth.com/blog/get-rid-of-tonsil-stones/ . Cryptolysis and tonsillectomy reported to help tonsillar halitosis (Ferguson 2014). Ben on camera: crypts, food and gunk squeezed into stones when you swallow (`RVXQmWSSfAo`, `p_rJ_7w4ME0`); check for a white spot or a scratchy stuck feeling (`xIa4b50z9vQ`); gargle nightly after they're out (`xIa4b50z9vQ`, `p_rJ_7w4ME0`); "laser them shut" or tonsillectomy (`RVXQmWSSfAo`, `p_rJ_7w4ME0`); a regular water flosser is too strong for the throat (`irSK2gbcOm4`).
19. **The stomach (the skeptic beat).** The lower esophageal sphincter "stays closed until you swallow, then opens so food can pass"; reflux is when it opens when it shouldn't. Cleveland Clinic: https://my.clevelandclinic.org/health/body/sphincters . ADA: "your bad breath could be the result of another problem, such as a sinus condition, gastric reflux, diabetes, liver or kidney disease" (row 13 page). Classification Type 1 oral, 2 airway, 3 gastroesophageal, 4 blood-borne, 5 subjective: Aydin & Harvey-Woodworth, Br Dent J 2014, https://www.nature.com/articles/sj.bdj.2014.552 . Postnasal drip "can decay on the back of the tongue" (Mayo diagnosis page, row 7, *search snippet*). Ben on camera: with bad diabetes breath "smells sweet because of the ketones" (`xIa4b50z9vQ`).
20. **When to see someone.** ADA: "If your dentist has ruled out other causes and you brush and floss every day, your bad breath could be the result of another problem"; "Bad breath that just won't go away or a constant bad taste in your mouth can be a warning sign of advanced gum disease." (row 13 page). Cleveland Clinic: "If you practice good hygiene and still have halitosis, schedule an appointment with a dentist" (row 6).
21. **About a week or two.** Tongue-cleaning trial, 30 people, measured at 2 weeks: the most-repetition group had the lowest odor scores (Gonçalves et al., J Appl Oral Sci 2019: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6442830/). Oral prophylaxis with tongue cleaning measured at 7 days (https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/30218226/). The "two weeks, nothing changed" rule is the brief's practical cut-off (question 6).
22. **Spit, don't rinse.** NHS: don't rinse right after brushing, it washes away the concentrated fluoride (summarized in https://www.dental.sa.gov.au/advice/spit-dont-rinse-teens-adults). Depends on the toothpaste having fluoride (question 5).
23. **Floss daily; cleanings.** ADA: brush twice daily, floss, regular checkups (row 13 page).

## Don't say (legal, invention and accuracy risks)
- "You cannot smell your own breath. That's not an opinion, that's how your brain works." (brief; thesis/antithesis tell, and "cannot" is absolute: the self-rating study found partial agreement). Say you're bad at it.
- "Scrape your tongue, don't brush it." Evidence is modest either way (row 11).
- "Alcohol mouthwash dries you out, which is a root cause." Mixed (row 13).
- "Sugary mints feed the bacteria [that cause bad breath]." Overstated (row 14); "your mints are making it worse" (Shorts idea) becomes "your mints aren't fixing it".
- Any "fixed for good" or cure claim for a product; any product claim Ben hasn't confirmed ("first ever", "patented", "top selling", "99%" reductions, study results). Say what each product doesn't do.
- "It's never your stomach." It can be (row 19).
- Stopping or switching a medicine without the prescriber.
- Specific tonsil-stone tools (picks, bobby pins, fingers) as advice.
- Brand names other than his own: "Q-tip" (say cotton swab), mouthwash and gum brands.
- Anything about a patient, a colleague, or Ben's own breath, routine or history unless he supplies it.
